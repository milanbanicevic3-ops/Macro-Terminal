Regime

The war is intensifying, not stabilizing. The US bombing campaign against Iran entered its 13th consecutive night Thursday, with no ceasefire in sight. Attacks have intensified further after Houthi forces struck two Saudi vessels in the Red Sea on Wednesday. Trump has warned Iran will be held responsible for any further Houthi action and has floated using frozen Iranian assets to cover shipping damages — rhetoric that, combined with a reported US troop buildup in the region this week, raises real questions about whether a threatened larger strike could actually follow.

Oil is the clearest, most direct market read on how seriously this is being taken. Brent is back above $100 a barrel for the first time since late May, up roughly 12% this week alone; WTI futures are up nearly 10% on the week and roughly 30% on the month. There's a real argument that even these moves understate the risk: reports suggest a fresh US ceasefire proposal was made this week and rejected by Iran, and markets may still be pricing some probability of a near-term diplomatic exit that hasn't materialized.

Yen stress has now drawn in a second official voice. USD/JPY approached 164 as the 10-year Treasury yield broke above 4.70% for the first time since January 2025. Japan's Finance Minister issued a fresh verbal intervention warning; separately, and more unusually, the US Treasury called yen volatility "undesirable" while explicitly urging the BoJ to hike. Two governments now on record about the same currency move in the same week is a genuine escalation in official attention, even without a concrete policy action yet.

Central bank stances
Fed

FOMC decision Wednesday, hold expected at 3.50–3.75%. The tone on September is the real event; markets currently price elevated odds of a hike given sticky inflation and oil pass-through.

ECB

Some genuine relief here. It turned out the ECB did not pre-signal a September hike as some had quietly expected, offering a small amount of breathing room even as officials remain concerned about inflation.

BoE

Decision Thursday, hold expected at 3.75%. UK retail sales got a genuine, if temporary, boost from warm weather and World Cup excitement in June — worth noting as color, not as a structural improvement in the fiscal-credibility concerns still weighing on sterling.

BoJ

Decision Friday, hold expected at 1.00%. Despite the yen's slide and the dual verbal intervention this week, investors have not meaningfully raised expectations for BoJ hikes — the policy divergence with the Fed, not a change in BoJ stance itself, is what's driving the yen weaker.

Trade policy

A new tariff regime replaced the prior one this week with little market reaction. Following the earlier court ruling against the original tariffs, the administration shifted to Trade Act authority to impose 10% and 12.5% tariffs on 60 countries, replacing the temporary tariffs that expired. This doesn't materially change the existing picture, though the administration retains authority to raise tariffs as high as 100% if it chooses to escalate further.

RBA / RBNZ / BoC

No fresh signals from any of the three this week. Australian CPI Wednesday is the nearest data point with direct policy relevance.

Scenario context: could this escalate to a ground campaign?

Worth understanding the shape of escalation risk, not as a prediction but as context for how bad this could get if the air campaign fails to achieve its goals. Analysis circulating this week lays out the realistic options and their costs: an internal-destabilization approach using Kurdish forces was reportedly considered earlier in the conflict and abandoned after Turkey signaled it wouldn't tolerate a strengthened Kurdish military presence; a strategic-islands approach (Kharg, Larak, and Qeshm, covering oil exports, Hormuz surveillance, and the Revolutionary Guard's main naval base respectively) is described as tactically feasible but likely to trap US forces in a prolonged defensive fight given proximity to the Iranian coast; and a full coastal invasion is described as requiring tens to hundreds of thousands of troops, with securing Bandar Abbas alone estimated at roughly 17,000 marines. The analysis is explicit that Iran's real strength lies in asymmetric warfare (drones, fast attack boats, decentralized command) rather than a conventional fight, and that any ground escalation risks drawing in Kuwait, Bahrain, and wider regional energy infrastructure. None of this is imminent, but it's the realistic tail-risk shape worth understanding rather than dismissing.
