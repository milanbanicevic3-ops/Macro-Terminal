Regime

The Fed held, as expected, but the market reaction was the real story. The statement described economic activity expanding at a "solid pace" with employment growth tracking labor force growth, and reaffirmed the 2% target. Three members dissented in favor of an immediate hike. Warsh's press conference was substantively hawkish — he repeatedly stressed inflation remains too high, said the Fed won't declare victory on one or two favorable CPI reports, and framed the three dissents as healthy internal debate rather than something to downplay, itself a signal that the hawkish wing is growing.

Despite that tone, the dollar weakened, and the mechanism matters. Markets had priced roughly 30–36% odds of an actual hike going in; getting only a hold with no forward guidance was, relative to that pricing, a disappointment. When pressed on why the Fed didn't hike given elevated market expectations, Warsh explained that financial conditions have already tightened meaningfully on rising bond yields and higher financing costs, and the Fed wants to assess that impact before acting further — in effect, letting the market do the tightening for now.

The yield curve reaction is the most important technical detail from this week, and it's not a growth story. Short-end yields fell while the 10, 20, and 30-year all pushed to levels last seen in 2008 — a pronounced bear-steepening. The read: markets aren't pricing stronger growth, they're demanding a bigger risk premium for holding long-dated debt given an active war, inflation still above target, and heavy new Treasury issuance. If the Fed doesn't eventually follow long yields higher, this divergence risks persisting or worsening, and it's the direct reason the desk's own multi-session USD-bullish stance broke this week.

Central bank stances — the full picture, resolved
Fed

Held at 3.50–3.75%, hawkish substance, dovish market reaction. September hike odds eased from fully priced pre-meeting to roughly 75% now — a real, if modest, softening worth tracking against any thesis that leans on imminent Fed tightening.

BoE

Held at 3.75%, but the vote split genuinely hardened: 6-3, up from 7-2 at the prior meeting. Three hawkish dissents rather than two is a real signal, not noise, and lands squarely in favor of GBP strength.

BoJ

Held at 1.00%, 8-1, with one dissent favoring a hike. The Bank upgraded its FY2026 growth forecast slightly while cutting the near-term inflation forecast, but explicitly reiterated readiness to raise rates further. The yen has still not meaningfully benefited from any of this hawkish rhetoric — USD/JPY remains just below 164.

Professional consensus into the Fed decision

Institutional bank surveys ahead of the meeting largely converge on what actually happened. Most major desks expected a hold with two hawkish dissents (Logan and Hammack by name), a possible third, and no forward guidance from Warsh — exactly the outcome delivered, down to three dissents. A minority of desks flagged real hike risk given the oil-driven inflation shock, with one house's base case still calling for hikes beginning as soon as September.

A published scenario framework, for context

One widely-read independent strategist (Brent Donnelly, Spectra Markets) laid out four FOMC scenarios ahead of the meeting and rated a genuinely non-consensus "neutral/dovish hike" as his own base case, with the actual "hawkish hold" outcome flagged as the market's base case and explicitly described as not a clean market mover given September was already fully priced. His framework anticipated real ambiguity in how bonds would react to any hike outcome specifically, which is a useful lens now that the actual result has produced exactly that kind of yield-curve ambiguity.

Analyst Conclusion
Dominant theme, primary risk, clearest setups

The dominant theme is that the market's reaction to this week's central bank triple-header mattered more than the decisions themselves. A hawkish-in-substance Fed hold produced dollar weakness because it fell short of elevated hike pricing. A genuine bear-steepening in the Treasury curve, with long-end yields at 2008-era levels, reads as a credibility question about the Fed's inflation-fighting resolve rather than a growth story, and it's serious enough that the desk's own eight-session USD-bullish, gold-sell framework broke this week. That reversal is stated plainly here rather than softened, because it's exactly the kind of real, reasoned change in view this process is built to catch and act on.

Two of three active setups earned genuine downgrades this session, both to Low, both on real, converging evidence rather than routine caution. GBP/USD faces a hawkened BoE vote split plus broad dollar weakness plus the strongest bullish momentum reading of the entire series, three independent forces pointing the same direction. NZD/USD faces the same broad-dollar headwind on top of its own already-flagged fundamental and technical pressures. USD/CAD holds at Low with very little room left before a genuine closure conversation becomes necessary, given oil, an unresolved central bank question, and now broad dollar weakness all working against it simultaneously.

The single most important open question heading into next week is whether this move is durable or a post-event overreaction. Next week's calendar is comparatively light, which makes it the right window to find out rather than forcing a conclusion today.
